### 14.1 Contribution Does Not Require Meeting Every Need

When you notice a possible improvement, an interesting opportunity and someone who needs help, the difficulty may be allocation rather than generosity. Your three defined groups offer a way to distinguish understanding, participation and support. One response need not expand into responsibility for the whole situation. You can suggest a method without managing implementation, take part in one stage without maintaining it indefinitely, or care about someone without agreeing to an arrangement beyond your capacity.

### 14.2 Time and Attention Are Real Resources

Before adding a commitment, consider it alongside responsibilities already in place. A request that adds only a little each day can become substantial over several weeks. Record the time, cooperation, costs and maintenance it will require. This does not reduce a relationship to an accounting exercise; it gives contribution a chance to continue. The chart supplies no budget figure. Financial decisions still require actual information about income, savings and expenditure.

### 14.3 Making Reciprocity Discussable

The support theme of 37–40 can become a clear discussion of reciprocity. Some contributions involve money; others involve time, care, knowledge or reliable cooperation. Ask what each person considers meaningful instead of assuming that everyone values the exchange in the same way. If one side repeatedly lacks support, examine whether the arrangement has become unbalanced and what could change. Shared terms are easier to work with than an expectation that the other person will understand an unspoken need.

### 14.4 Preserving the Capacity to Choose

An experience does not have to become income, a career or a permanent commitment to be valuable. Some reveal a limit; some collaborations make sense for one stage. Allowing an ending can preserve room for further learning and choice. This chapter concerns how resources are used, not financial destiny or investment outcomes. A direction worth retaining should be examined through practical conditions, personal willingness and the continuing cost of participation.

### 14.5 Making a Helpful Contribution Sustainable

You might agree to help with a shared project each week, then discover that communication, preparation and follow-up have also become your responsibility. List the actual work before discussing what could rotate, what could be reduced and what exceeds the original agreement. You need not wait until exhaustion to name the issue. Considering contribution alongside resources can give goodwill a workable form and help you decide which collaborations are worth continuing.

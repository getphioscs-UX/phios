## 16.1 Turning an Interpretation into a Checkable Question

You have now encountered interpretations involving response, emotional decisions, expression, participation and support. Reality comparison is not an exercise in proving them correct. It asks whether they help you understand an actual situation. Choose a recent question without urgent risk, such as whether to accept an additional work responsibility. Record what was known, what interested you, what others expected and what information was missing. Do not supply events that never occurred; retain uncertainty where a detail cannot be recalled.


## 16.2 Comparing Interest with Commitment

Compare what first interested you with the responsibility you eventually accepted. If defining the scope helped you remain involved, that may support the practical suggestion to distinguish interest from commitment. If you made a clear choice promptly and sustained it under similar conditions, retain that counterexample. Skills, workload and team support may also explain the outcome. One experience cannot establish that a Type caused your behavior.

## 16.4 Support, Challenge and Inconclusive Results

Use three possible outcomes: specific observations support the suggestion, a meaningful counterexample challenges it, or the information remains inconclusive. Liking a description does not count as support, and one difficult result does not settle every question. Record what happened, what else could explain it and what could be compared next time. No completed personal observation has been generated for you. If you have not recorded an experience, the honest starting point is that the interpretation has not yet been assessed.


## 16.5 Keeping the Next Review Small and Specific

Choose one adjustable practice, such as confirming the scope of a new task in writing. Set a review point that fits your schedule, then examine whether the practice reduced misunderstanding or helped you deliver what you agreed. If it did not help, you may stop using it. There is no need to demonstrate that it must apply to you. The reading can become a revisable thinking tool, while the evidence continues to come from your life.


## Use One Real Question to Test the Reading’s Usefulness

Choose one current question, such as whether to join a project. Record the tasks, time available, expectations and what remains unpromised. List important unknowns: the actual workload, support and whether your willingness changes over time. The chart can organize these questions but cannot supply an outcome. A later review can then distinguish the original interpretation, your choice and information learned afterward.


## Keep Support, Counterexamples and Unclear Outcomes

At review, separate observations that support an idea, counterexamples and outcomes that remain unclear. Perhaps you expected expanding responsibility, yet a clear agreement kept the task within scope. That is useful information for revising the earlier interpretation. Keep the date, context and remaining unknowns. Do not remove counterexamples to make the report appear correct or turn a single event into a final judgment.

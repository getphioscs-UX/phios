Interwar Economic Crisis and Extreme Mobilization

The interwar global economy reconnected trade, capital and domestic politics in unstable ways. The systemic crisis after 1929 intensified unemployment, financial stress and policy pressure, while fascist movements converted social dislocation into highly concentrated political mobilization.

Why Population Compression First Becomes a Problem of Replacing Critical Nodes

Population compression begins in the structure of age, capability, and responsibility rather than in the headline population number. Fewer younger nodes and more older nodes rearrange labor, care, public finance, and knowledge renewal. The economy and cities may continue to function through existing adults, capital, and technology, while the real change occurs in the inflow of future nodes: more present capability is used to maintain what already exists, leaving less surplus for the next stage.

How Civilization Builds an Attention Infrastructure: Networks, Infrastructure, and Collective Sensing

Large-scale coordination depends on more than information existing; signals must move through a network, ride on infrastructure, be integrated across distributed participants, and compete for limited attention. Network runtime, infrastructure runtime, collective neural networks, the attention economy, and attention competition together describe the physical and cognitive channels through which civilization senses and responds.

How Finance Pulls the Future into the Present: Debt, Capital, Narrative, and Speculation

Finance reorganizes time. Debt commits future output, capital allocation concentrates resources around selected possibilities, financial narratives coordinate expectations, speculative cycles amplify reinforcing signals, and financial compression converts a wide field of uncertainty into a smaller set of actionable indicators. The system gains the ability to act ahead of completed reality, but it also accumulates assumptions about what the future must provide.

Understanding BaZi: from eight characters to one structure

The diagrams below explain the method; your calculated chart appears in the chart section above.

Why four pillars and eight characters?

Each of the birth year, month, day and hour is expressed as a stem–branch pair. These four positions are the Four Pillars; their eight characters give BaZi its name. They are calendrical positions, rather than eight labels attached directly to the digits of a civil date. Calculation establishes the structure before interpretation begins.

How do the four positions make a chart?

Read the year, month, day and hour positions first, then distinguish the stems above from the branches below. The same symbol in another position does not have exactly the same relationships. The month provides seasonal context; the day stem supplies the Day Master reference. If the birth time is unknown, hour-dependent information remains uncertain rather than being filled in for visual completeness.

What do stems, branches, yin–yang and five phases do?

Stems and branches are two sets of symbols used to organize cycles and structure. Yin and yang distinguish qualities within that language. Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal and Water describe generating and controlling relationships. Hidden stems describe the internal composition associated with a branch. A branch is therefore more than an animal name, and a phase is not a complete personality conclusion.

How do the Day Master and Ten Gods enter a reading?

The Day Master is the stem of the day pillar. The Ten Gods name relationships between that reference and other stems, using phase and polarity. Generating me, generated by me, controlling me, controlled by me and sharing my phase form five basic relationships; polarity differentiates the ten roles. Names such as Wealth, Officer and Resource belong to this relational vocabulary. They do not directly establish an amount of money, a job title or a particular person.

Why is one character not enough?

Two charts can both contain Wood while differing in season, position, support and constraint. Saying only “much Wood” skips those differences. Selecting one Ten-God role also overlooks whether it is supported or constrained. A coherent reading considers the Day Master, seasonal context, stem–branch relationships and available information together, showing the structure behind an interpretation.

How do timing layers relate to the natal chart?

The natal chart is the four-pillar structure calculated from birth information. Da Yun and annual layers introduce other time positions. Reading them means examining their relationships with the natal chart, rather than replacing it. Which layers are available depends on the supplied information and the current reading scope. A calculated relationship still needs to be considered alongside lived experience and circumstances; it is not an inevitable event.

Where should you look first on a chart?

Identify the year, month, day and hour labels, then locate the stem in the day column: the Day Master. Tools can display the columns in opposite directions, so do not identify it by counting from the left. Distinguish branches from hidden stems before exploring phase relationships, Ten Gods and timing. When comparing charts, first check the birth record, time-zone and calendar settings rather than comparing colours.

Ten stems and twelve branches: two distinct cycles

The ten stems are Jia, Yi, Bing, Ding, Wu, Ji, Geng, Xin, Ren and Gui. The twelve branches are Zi, Chou, Yin, Mao, Chen, Si, Wu, Wei, Shen, You, Xu and Hai. Their ordered pairing forms the traditional sixty-pair cycle. Each pillar has its own calculated pair; the year animal does not fill all four columns. Jia and Yi belong to Wood, Bing and Ding to Fire, Wu and Ji to Earth, Geng and Xin to Metal, and Ren and Gui to Water. Polarity differentiates the two stems within each phase, so a phase label alone does not describe the whole position.

Hidden stems: reading the composition within a branch

Hidden stems are the stems associated with a branch under the existing method. For example, Ren and Jia appear within Hai. They do not add two new pillars, and their location differs from a stem shown directly above a branch. First identify the branch containing them, then read their roles relative to the actual Day Master alongside season, position and other relationships. A phase appearing in a visible stem and the same phase appearing in a hidden stem therefore occupy different positions. Early background: observing time and nature

The existing background material traces the foundations to astronomy, calendars and yin–yang and five-phase thought. Organizing seasons, cycles and everyday experience encouraged a language of change and relationships. These foundations supplied later methods with temporal and relational concepts; they should not be confused with a fully formed modern Four Pillars system.

Han-era background: cycles and calendrical frameworks

In the Han-era stage described by the fixed introduction, stem–branch and related calendrical frameworks developed further. For later Four Pillars practice, the important contribution is a shared way to record and compare time rather than an arbitrary symbol assigned to each date. Place, time and calendrical conventions remain important when using a chart calculator today.

Tang evidence: Li Xuzhong and birth-time interpretation

Han Yu’s epitaph for Li Xuzhong provides an important account of Tang-era practice, describing reasoning from birth year, month and day, stems and branches, and five-phase relationships. Books attributed to Li became part of the tradition’s transmission. The Siku synopsis of Li Xuzhong Ming Shu, however, identifies material from different periods in the surviving text. This stage helps explain the tradition of organizing a reading through time and relationships, while keeping later additions distinct from an author’s own work.

Song transmission: four pillars and the Ziping name

The Siku edition of Luoluzi Sanming Xiaoxi Fu Zhu attributes its commentary to Xu Ziping of the Song period. Its synopsis discusses interpretation using the eight characters of year, month, day and hour, and the later use of “Ziping” as a name for this tradition. It also notes that Xu’s biography is not securely known. The text documents an expanding four-pillar and commentary tradition, rather than establishing an exact inventor-and-date genealogy. Later approaches to the Day Master, positions and chart relationships should be read alongside the texts they use.

Ming compilation: Wan Minying and Sanming Tonghui

The National Central Library’s union catalogue records Sanming Tonghui as a twelve-volume work by the Ming author Wan Minying. It brings together multiple areas of Ziping practice; the Siku synopsis discusses its treatment of roles such as Officer, Resource, Wealth and Output, and of useful factors. Compilation made different discussions easier to consult and compare. For readers, the practical lesson is to connect a term with its source, assumptions and role in the whole chart.

Qing editorial work: reading texts and their editions

The Siku synopses compare author attributions, volume counts, surviving versions and later additions as well as listing titles. Their discussions of Li Xuzhong Ming Shu, Luoluzi Sanming Xiaoxi Fu Zhu and Sanming Tonghui show a tradition transmitted through changing texts and commentaries. Editions and interpretive emphases matter. When two claims appear to conflict, first identify their texts, commentaries and contexts before deciding how they can be compared.

Today: calculation tools and real-life comparison

Digital tools make calculation and reference easier, but speed does not replace input precision or provide real-life evidence for an interpretation. PHI OS begins with the structure supported by its existing calculation, then organizes themes for comparison with your experience, circumstances and choices. The traditional vocabulary becomes an entry into observation rather than a decision about your future.

Structure and reading: follow the relationships layer by layer

Locate the four pillars before distinguishing stems, branches and hidden stems. The Day Master supplies a reference and the month supplies seasonal context; other positions and relationships still belong in the same reading.

Direct and indirect role names are not a ranking of good and bad. Read each role through its position, connections and the chart’s support and constraints.

How PHI OS reads: from structure back to life

1. Understand the calculated structure

Confirm the person, birth details and time precision before exploring the Day Master, seasonal context and pillar positions. Use this page’s structural guide for unfamiliar terms. The first aim is to understand what the chart represents and what remains uncertain, rather than immediately labelling yourself.

2. Read themes in context

Move from the overview into the themes actually supplied by the report, such as relationships, work, resources or timing. Notice which parts of the structure support an interpretation, its conditions and the surrounding explanation. A statement in one chapter should not be detached from its context and applied to every situation.

3. Compare with experience and circumstances

Bring a theme back to situations you can describe: in which environments have you observed it, and what examples point the other way? Does it change when resources, responsibilities, relationships or opportunities change? These questions connect an abstract structure with reality and leave room to question an interpretation instead of requiring you to fit the report.

4. Form questions worth observing

A useful next step can be concrete: under which conditions do I work well, what choices do I have when a familiar pressure returns, and what information is still missing? Write down a question and use observation and action to learn more. A report supplies material for reflection; decisions still need your circumstances, information and judgement.
